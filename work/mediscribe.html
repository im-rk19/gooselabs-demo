The problem

Clinical documentation is the most valuable data a practice produces and the most painful to produce.

Doctors write notes between patients or after clinic hours. Under time pressure, notes compress into personal shorthand that only the writer can reliably decode. The information is technically recorded and practically unusable — so six months later, nobody can answer whether a treatment protocol worked across a cohort.

What MediScribe does

It listens to the consultation and produces a structured clinical note.

Not a transcript — a transcript is just a longer version of the problem. MediScribe separates history, examination findings, assessment and plan into an EMR-ready structure, and handles the mixed Hindi-English speech that is normal in Indian consultations rather than assuming clean English.

The design constraint that shaped everything

The system cannot add time to a consultation. If a doctor has to correct a note for three minutes, they will stop using it and go back to shorthand within a week.

So MediScribe is built around review rather than authorship: the doctor reads and confirms rather than writes. Where the system is uncertain, it flags rather than guesses — a note with three flagged uncertainties is far safer than a confident note with three silent errors.

Clinical safety

The clinician remains the author. Nothing enters the record without review and confirmation. This is not a hedge — automated clinical documentation without a human confirmation step is a patient safety problem, and we would not build it.

What made it hard

Indian consultations switch language mid-sentence, use local terms for symptoms, and happen in rooms with corridor noise. Off-the-shelf models trained on clean English clinical audio degrade sharply under those conditions.

Most of the engineering went into the messy reality rather than the model.
