The client

Young Indians is a national movement under CII, and the Raipur chapter has over three hundred members. Alongside networking it runs philanthropy work, climate initiatives and road safety drives, plus a steady calendar of events.

It is run entirely by members who have full-time jobs.

The problem

Every operational task ran through WhatsApp groups, and each one consumed volunteer hours.

RSVPs were counted by tallying reactions in a group of hundreds. Attendance was marked on printed sheets and rarely entered anywhere afterwards. The member directory went stale within a quarter. Member offers reached whoever happened to be scrolling. Annual reporting was reconstructed from memory and photographs.

None of it was hard. All of it was constant, and it fell on people who had already done a day's work.

What we built

WhatsOn started deliberately small: events, RSVPs and QR check-ins. Those three functions covered where volunteer time was actually going.

Proper event listings with confirmed RSVPs, so organisers know numbers before catering is ordered. QR check-in that takes about a second at the door and produces attendance data as a by-product. A member directory members maintain themselves. Offers and announcements that reach the whole chapter without another group. Birthdays — which in a membership community drive more engagement than any feature list suggests. And engagement analytics for the chapter leadership.

The decision that mattered

We resisted building a social feed. It was requested, it would have been the most visible feature, and it would almost certainly have been empty within a month — because members already have WhatsApp for conversation.

Building the three unglamorous things well was the right call.

Why this generalises

Every membership organisation in India has this problem. The Rotary clubs, EO and YPO chapters, trade associations and alumni bodies all run on volunteer time and WhatsApp groups.

WhatsOn is the reference build for that pattern.
