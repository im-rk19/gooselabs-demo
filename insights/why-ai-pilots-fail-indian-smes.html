Why AI pilots stall in Indian mid-market businesses

Palash Kanwar · 14 Jul 2026 · 6 min read

There is a specific kind of meeting that happens six months after an AI pilot. Everyone agrees the demo was impressive. Nobody can point to anything that changed. The project is quietly not renewed.

I have now watched this closely enough to say the failure is rarely technical. The model usually worked.

1. The pilot solved a problem nobody was losing sleep over

Use cases get chosen because they are technically interesting or because a competitor announced something similar. Neither is a reason.

The test worth applying: if this worked perfectly, whose week gets better, and would that person notice by Friday? If the answer requires a paragraph, the use case is wrong.

2. It required people to change where they work

Adoption dies at the login screen. A tool that lives in a new dashboard competes with WhatsApp, and WhatsApp wins because WhatsApp is already open.

This is why we build so much into WhatsApp itself. Not because it is elegant, but because the alternative does not get used.

3. The data was not there

Ambitious pilots frequently assume data the business does not actually have — not in the sense of it being messy, but in the sense of it never having been recorded.

Messy data is a work item. Non-existent data is a different project, usually a longer one, and it needs to be named at the start rather than discovered in month three.

4. Nobody owned it after launch

A pilot is delivered, the vendor leaves, and it belongs to nobody. The first time it produces a wrong output, there is no one to correct it, so people stop trusting it. Trust does not recover.

Every system needs a named internal owner before it launches. If nobody will take that role, that is a signal about how much the organisation actually wants it.

5. Success was never defined

Ask what the pilot needs to achieve to be continued and you often get "see if AI can help us". That is not a criterion, so at review time the discussion is about impressions rather than evidence.

Agree the number before building. Even a rough one changes the conversation at month six.

What we do differently

Our discovery sprints are designed to produce a no when a no is right. A client who does not build this quarter but builds the correct thing next year is a better outcome than one who spends money on a pilot that quietly dies.

That is a slightly awkward position for a company that gets paid to build things. It is also the reason the builds we do take on tend to survive.
