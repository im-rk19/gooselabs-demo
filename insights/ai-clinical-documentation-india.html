What AI clinical documentation actually has to get right in India

Palash Kanwar · 21 Apr 2026 · 7 min read

Ambient clinical documentation is one of the few AI applications in healthcare with an obvious case. Doctors spend a great deal of time writing notes. The notes are essential. Writing them is not what anyone trained for.

The demos are impressive. Deploying one in an Indian clinic is a different exercise, and the gap is not where people expect.

Consultations are not in one language

A consultation in Raipur moves between Hindi and English within a sentence, and sometimes into Chhattisgarhi. Symptoms are described in local terms. Drug names are pronounced in ways no training set anticipated.

Speech models trained largely on clean English clinical audio degrade sharply here, and they degrade quietly — producing confident, fluent, wrong text rather than obviously failing.

The room is loud

Clinical audio in the demo is a quiet consulting room with a good microphone. Actual audio has corridor noise, a fan, a phone, and a family member of the patient contributing.

Most of our engineering effort went here rather than into the model.

A transcript is not a note

Handing a doctor a verbatim transcript makes the problem longer. A clinical note is a structured artefact — history, examination, assessment, plan — and the value is entirely in that structure.

Getting the structure right requires the system to understand which part of a rambling conversation is history and which is assessment. That is harder than transcription and it is the part that determines whether the output is usable.

The review step is the product

The design constraint that matters more than accuracy: the system cannot add time to the consultation.

If a doctor spends three minutes correcting a note, they stop using it inside a week and go back to shorthand. So the interaction has to be review-and-confirm, not read-and-rewrite.

This also means uncertainty must be surfaced rather than smoothed over. A note flagging three uncertain items is far safer than a fluent note with three silent errors — and clinicians calibrate their trust accordingly, which is exactly what you want.

Clinical safety is not negotiable

The clinician is the author. Nothing enters the record without human confirmation. Automated clinical documentation without a review step is a patient safety issue, and no efficiency argument outweighs that.

Data residency

Patient data should stay on infrastructure the clinic controls, in India. This constrains architecture — including which models can be used and how — and it is far cheaper to design for at the start than to retrofit after a compliance review.

Where this goes

Once notes are structured rather than shorthand, the second-order benefits arrive: cohort analysis, protocol comparison, real answers to whether a treatment approach worked.

That is the actual prize. Saving the doctor an hour is what gets the system adopted.
