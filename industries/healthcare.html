Healthcare & Diagnostics

Clinics, hospitals, diagnostics and longevity practices.

What we keep finding in this sector

  • Patient information split across paper files, WhatsApp images and spreadsheets
  • Doctors writing clinical notes after clinic hours instead of seeing patients
  • Follow-ups that depend on whether the front desk remembers
  • No reliable view of whether treatment programmes are working

We build healthcare from inside a clinic

GooseLabs' healthcare practice did not start with a market study. It started at Prānaa, a regenerative medicine and longevity clinic in Raipur where our founder is technology lead, and where every system we describe has to survive a real Tuesday morning.

That is an unusual position for a technology vendor and it changes what we build. We have watched good software fail because it added fifteen seconds to a consultation. We design against that now.

What the sector actually struggles with

Indian clinics are not short of technology. Most run billing software, a lab system, perhaps a booking tool. What they lack is anything connecting them, which means the patient record exists in five places and matches in none.

The second problem is documentation. Clinical notes are the highest-value data a practice generates and the most painful to produce, so they get compressed into shorthand that only the writing doctor can decode. Six months later, nobody can tell whether a treatment protocol worked.

Where we usually start

Documentation, for consultation-heavy practices — an AI assistant that listens and produces structured, EMR-ready notes.

Intake and diagnostics, for practices where patients arrive with test results in a dozen formats.

Programme adherence, for practices selling multi-month journeys, where the commercial risk is a patient quietly dropping out in week five.

On patient data

Health data sits on infrastructure the clinic controls, in India. We treat this as a design constraint from the first sketch rather than a compliance step at the end.
